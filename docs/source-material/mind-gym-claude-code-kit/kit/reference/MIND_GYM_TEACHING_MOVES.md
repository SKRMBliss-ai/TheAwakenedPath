# Mind Gym — Teaching Moves

**How to hand a child a complex idea without ever explaining it.**

**Owner:** Sim · **Last updated:** 2 September 2026
**Companion to:** `MIND_GYM_CONCEPT_TRANSLATION.md` (which concepts) · `MIND_GYM_MASTER_PLAN.md` (where they land)
**This document replaces the flat child-facing copy in the translation library.**

---

## The rule everything here follows

**Never explain an inner experience to a child. Build a moment where they catch it happening.**

A child who is *told* that squashing feelings doesn't work has learned a sentence. A child who has just tried not to laugh and failed has learned the thing itself, in their body, permanently. The second child never needs reminding.

So every concept below arrives as one of three moves:

| Move | What it is | Why it works |
|------|-----------|--------------|
| **The experiment** | Something the child does that proves the point by what happens | The child is the evidence. Nothing to take on trust |
| **The borrowed image** | A thing from the child's own world that already has the right shape | No new understanding required — just recognition |
| **The trapdoor** | An instruction that fails on purpose, and the failure *is* the lesson | Delight. Children love being caught out by something true |

Three things this document never does: state the moral at the end, ask a child to take something on faith, or use a metaphor from an adult life (mortgages, roommates, commutes, work stress).

## 1 · You cannot squash a feeling

*Replaces: "You don't have to squash it. Feelings that get squashed just wait around."*

### The move — a trapdoor · ages 3–8 and 9–14

> **"Quick — try not to laugh."**
>
> *(a pause, then something mildly silly happens on screen)*
>
> **"How did that go?"**

Then, only after they've felt it:

> **"Funny thing about feelings. The harder you hold one down, the harder it pushes back up. Same as trying not to laugh in assembly. Same as trying not to cry when someone asks if you're okay."**

### Why this one works

Every child on earth has failed to not-laugh. It's universal, it's funny, and it's the exact mechanism — the effort of suppression is what feeds the thing. No child has to believe us; they've already run the experiment a hundred times.

### The second version, for water

> **"Next time you're in the bath, try to hold a ball under the water. Keep it down there. How long can you do it? What happens the second you let go?"**
>
> **"That's what holding a feeling down is like. It takes all your strength — and it comes back up anyway."**

An off-screen mission, so it lands in real life rather than in an app.

### What we no longer say
~~"Let it be there — it'll go on its own."~~ True, but it's an instruction. The child has nothing to do with it.

## 2 · There is a voice in your head

*Replaces: "Your brain is a talker."*

### The move — a trapdoor · both bands

> **"Go quiet inside your head. Completely quiet. No thoughts at all. Ten seconds. Starting… now."**
>
> *(ten seconds of a quiet screen)*
>
> **"So. Did it stay quiet?"**
>
> **"Something started talking, didn't it. Probably about how this was a weird thing to ask you to do."**
>
> **"That's the thing we're looking for. It never stops. It's talking right now, about this."**

### Why this one works

The instruction is impossible and is *meant* to fail. The child discovers the inner voice by trying to switch it off — which is the only way anyone ever notices it. There's also a small joke in it: the voice's first act is to complain about the exercise, which the app predicts correctly, and children find that delightful.

### The guard dog — for the times it's shouting

> **"Your brain has a guard dog. Its whole job is to bark if something's coming."**
>
> **"Sometimes it's right. Mostly it's the postman."**
>
> **"You wouldn't get rid of the dog. It's trying to look after you. You just learn to check — postman, or actually something?"**

Carries three things at once: the voice is separate from you, it's *protective* rather than broken, and it's often wrong without being bad. Children who have never met a guard dog have met a barking dog.

## 3 · You are the one noticing

*The hardest idea in the app. Replaces: "You're the one who notices."*

### The move — an experiment · both bands

> **"Say this out loud: 'I am cross.'"**
>
> **"Now say this: 'I notice I am cross.'"**
>
> **"Say them again. Feel the difference?"**
>
> **"In the second one there are two of you. The cross one — and the one who noticed."**

### Why this one works

It's not an argument, it's a demonstration in the child's own mouth. The shift is small and physical and nearly everyone feels it. Nothing has to be believed.

### For younger children — the scary film

> **"When you watch a scary bit in a film, you get scared. Properly scared."**
>
> **"But you also know you're sitting on the sofa."**
>
> **"Both at once! The scared you, and the you that knows where the sofa is. That second one is always there, even when a feeling is really big."**

Children live this constantly and have never once had it pointed out.

## 4 · A thought is not an instruction

*Replaces: "Your brain said 'push him.' You didn't push him."*

### The move — an experiment · both bands

> **"Right now, think about hopping."**
>
> **"Really picture it. Hopping on one leg."**
>
> **"…Are you hopping?"**
>
> **"No. Thinking and doing are two different machines. Your brain can say anything it likes. Your legs are yours."**

### Why this one works

Instant, silly, and completely convincing. It also quietly does safeguarding work: a child who has had a frightening or unkind thought learns here that thinking it didn't make them anything. That's a shame-remover disguised as a joke.

## 5 · Some thoughts stick

*Replaces: "Some thoughts float away like bubbles. Some stick like glue."*

### The move — a borrowed image · ages 3–8

> **"You know when you've got a wobbly tooth?"**
>
> **"And your tongue keeps going back to it. Poke. Poke. Even when you've decided to stop."**
>
> **"Some thoughts are wobbly teeth."**

### The move — a trapdoor · ages 9–14

> **"For the next ten seconds, do not think about a purple elephant."**
>
> **"Whatever you do. No purple elephant."**
>
> *(ten seconds)*
>
> **"Yeah. Telling yourself not to think about something is a really good way to think about it."**

### Why these work

The wobbly tooth is a physical memory almost every child has, and it contains the whole idea: something you keep returning to, against your own intention, without meaning any harm. The purple elephant proves the same thing in ten seconds for older children, and it's the one demonstration in psychology that never fails.

## 6 · Urges rise and fall

*Replaces: "The want-to-shout feeling gets big… then smaller… then it's gone."*

### The move — an experiment · both bands

> **"Find an itch. Anywhere. Your arm, your nose, your ankle."**
>
> **"Now don't scratch it."**
>
> **"Just watch it. Don't fight it, don't scratch it. Watch what it does."**
>
> *(fifteen seconds)*
>
> **"It got worse. Then it got weird. Then it went — without you doing anything."**
>
> **"Wanting to shout does exactly that. So does wanting to hit something, or run away, or say the mean thing. It gets huge, and then it goes, all by itself, if you can just stay there while it does."**

### Why this one works

A child can run this right now, for free, and it takes fifteen seconds. It's the mechanism of urge-surfing with none of the vocabulary. And the child has now *had the experience* of an overwhelming pull passing on its own — which is the whole thing the Pause Room is trying to teach.

## 7 · Feelings pass

*Replaces: "Feelings are like weather. Storms pass."* — the weather image stays, but this goes in front of it.

### The move — an experiment · ages 9–14

> **"What were you upset about three Tuesdays ago?"**
>
> *(pause)*
>
> **"No. Me neither."**
>
> **"At the time it was the whole world. Now you can't even find it."**

### The move — a borrowed image · ages 3–8

> **"When you were little — really little — you used to cry about the blue cup."**
>
> **"Someone gave you the red cup, and it was the worst thing that had ever happened."**
>
> **"Do you still mind about the blue cup?"**

### Why these work

Both hand the child evidence from their own life, which is the only evidence a child can't argue with. Crucially neither says "so it doesn't matter" — the blue cup genuinely *was* the worst thing at the time, and the app says so. It's about time passing, never about the feeling being silly.

**Safety note:** never deployed while a child is in the middle of something. "This will pass" said too early is dismissal, and children hear it as *stop bothering me*. This lands in reflection, or on a calm day — never at step 1.

## 8 · Your mind writes to protect you

*Replaces: "Your brain is trying to keep you safe."*

### The move — a borrowed image · ages 3–8

> **"Have you ever burnt toast and set off the smoke alarm?"**
>
> **"Screaming. The whole house. For toast."**
>
> **"Was the alarm being naughty? No — it was doing its job. It just can't tell the difference between toast and a fire."**
>
> **"Your brain's got one of those. It goes off loud, about things that turn out to be toast. It's not broken. It's just very keen."**

### Ages 9–14

> **"That story your mind wrote — it came from the part of you whose entire job is spotting danger early."**
>
> **"It went for the worst version because the worst version is the one that would hurt if it caught you by surprise."**
>
> **"That's not you being negative. That's a smoke alarm doing exactly what it's for."**

### Why this matters more than any other move here

This is the screen where a child decides whether to keep being honest with the app. If step 6 ever lands as *you thought the wrong thing*, they will start giving safe answers at step 3 and the whole path becomes theatre. The smoke alarm makes the fear story **understandable and slightly funny** rather than shameful — which is the only tone that keeps a child telling the truth.

## 9 · Two feelings at once

*Replaces: "Can you feel happy and worried at the same time? Yes, you can!"*

### The move — a borrowed image · ages 3–8

> **"A wobbly tooth. You want it out — and you don't want it out."**
>
> **"At the exact same time. Both true."**
>
> **"Feelings do that. They come in pairs sometimes."**

### Ages 9–14

> **"The last day of a holiday."**
>
> **"You're not happy, and you're not sad. You're both, fully, at once — and neither one cancels the other."**
>
> **"That's not you being confused. That's just how people are built."**

### Why these work

The wobbly tooth is genuinely, physically ambivalent — a small child has *lived* wanting and dreading the same event. The last day of a holiday is the cleanest example of mixed emotion in a young person's life. Neither requires a child to accept that two feelings can coexist; both remind them of a time it already happened.

## 10 · What happened, and what your brain added

*The camera test — already strong. This sharpens it.*

### The move — an experiment · ages 3–8

> **"Pretend you've got a camera. Point it at what happened."**
>
> **"'Sam took the red car.' — Click. Got it. The camera saw that."**
>
> **"'Sam doesn't like me any more.' — Point the camera at that."**
>
> **"…You can't, can you? There's nothing there to point at."**
>
> **"That bit didn't happen out there. That bit happened in here."** *(taps head)*
>
> **"Both bits are allowed. They're just different kinds of bits."**

### Ages 9–14

> **"Write down what happened, exactly as a security camera would have recorded it. No feelings, no reasons, no 'obviously'."**
>
> **"Now write down everything else you know about it."**
>
> **"That second list is longer, isn't it. That's the bit your mind made. It might be right. But it's a different thing from the first list."**

### Why this works

"Can you point a camera at it?" is a test a four-year-old can genuinely run, which "is it true?" is not — and "is it true?" also sounds like an accusation, which is fatal here. The camera is neutral equipment. It doesn't judge, it just can't see certain things.

## 11 · More than one story fits

*Replaces: "Maybe Sam really wanted a turn."*

### The move — an experiment · ages 3–8

> **"Someone walked past you and didn't say hello."**
>
> **"Story one: they're cross with you."**
>
> **"Now let's make three more. Ready? Go."**
>
> *(child builds: they didn't see me · they were in a rush · they were sad about something · they were thinking about dinner)*
>
> **"Four stories. Same walking past. Which one is true?"**
>
> **"…We don't know! That's the interesting bit. We don't actually know."**

### Ages 9–14

> **"Your mind picked a story in about a tenth of a second and then stopped looking."**
>
> **"Find two more that fit everything you actually know. Not nicer ones — just ones that also fit."**
>
> **"You don't have to believe any of them. You just have to notice your mind had options and only showed you one."**

### Why this works

The child generates the alternatives — the app never supplies the "right" one. And it lands on *we don't know*, which is honest, whereas landing on a happier story would be the app telling a child how to feel. Uncertainty is the actual answer, and children can hold it perfectly well.

## 12 · Where a feeling sits in your body

*Replaces the flat "where do you notice it?"*

### The move — an experiment · ages 3–8

> **"Jump up and down. Ten times. Go on, properly."**
>
> *(ten jumps)*
>
> **"Now stop. Stand still. What's your heart doing?"**
>
> **"You can feel it, can't you? You just found something happening inside you."**
>
> **"That's the game. Feelings do that too — they show up somewhere in there. Now we know you can feel it."**

### Why this one is important

Before asking a child to detect something subtle, prove the equipment works. Jumping creates a sensation so obvious that nobody fails, and the child now knows what "noticing inside" feels like. Ask a four-year-old to find a feeling in their body cold and many will find nothing and quietly conclude they're bad at this.

### The essential caveat, in child words

> **"Where do you feel it? Tummy? Chest? Face? Somewhere else?"**
>
> **"There's no right place. My cross lives in my jaw. Someone else's lives in their hands. Yours lives wherever yours lives."**

## 13 · Loud isn't true

*Replaces: "Some thoughts shout. Shouting doesn't make them right."*

### The move — a borrowed image · both bands

> **"In a shop, the loudest person is not usually the one who knows the most."**

Then:

> **"Same in your head. The thought that keeps hammering at you — the one that's practically shouting — that's not the reliable one. It's just the loudest one."**
>
> **"Loud and right are different things."**

### Ages 9–14 — with evidence

> **"Your mind was completely certain everyone would notice. Absolutely sure."**
>
> **"Did they?"**
>
> **"Keep score for a week. Write down what it predicts and what actually happens. It's a genuinely surprising list."**

### Why this works

The scorekeeping version is powerful because it hands a pre-teen actual data about their own mind, and pre-teens trust data they gathered over anything an adult tells them. It also never says "you're wrong a lot" — it's the *prediction* being checked, never the child.

## 14 · You are kinder to other people

*The strongest single move in the app for 9–14.*

### The move — a trapdoor

> **"Your best friend comes to you and says: 'I'm so stupid. I ruined everything. Everyone thinks I'm pathetic.'"**
>
> **"What do you say to them?"**
>
> *(child answers — and children are almost always warm, specific and kind here)*
>
> **"Nice. Really nice, actually."**
>
> **"Now — that thought was yours. From earlier. You said it about yourself an hour ago."**
>
> **"So… why is what you just said good enough for them and not for you?"**

### Why this works

The trapdoor is the whole point. The child gives their kindest, most natural answer *before* discovering it applies to them, so it can't be dismissed as being nice to yourself on purpose. It's their own voice, their own words, handed back. Nothing an adult could say lands within a mile of this.

**Handle with care:** never used as a gotcha or with any sense of "aha, caught you." The tone is warm and slightly wry. If it lands as clever, it fails.

## 15 · Anger is often standing in front of something else

*For the Big Feelings Room.*

### The move — a borrowed image · ages 3–8

> **"When a dog is scared, what does it do?"**
>
> **"It barks. Big and loud and fierce. So you think it's angry."**
>
> **"It's not angry. It's scared, standing in front of the scared with something loud."**
>
> **"People do that too. Sometimes the shouty feeling has a smaller, softer one hiding behind it."**

### Ages 9–14

> **"Anger's very good at getting there first."**
>
> **"Have a look behind it. Nine times out of ten there's something quieter back there — hurt, or embarrassed, or scared, or left out."**
>
> **"Anger isn't lying to you. It's standing in front of the thing that's harder to say."**

### Why this works

Never tells a child their anger is *really* something else, which is invalidating and instantly rejected. Anger is real, and it's doing something useful — arriving first, taking the hit. The child is invited to look behind it, not to give it up.

## 16 · The spotlight isn't on you

*For embarrassment, 9–14.*

### The move — a trapdoor

> **"Think of something embarrassing you did. Not the worst one. A medium one."**
>
> **"Got it? Right. Now — think of something embarrassing that someone else did, in front of you, this year."**
>
> *(long pause)*
>
> **"Struggling?"**
>
> **"Everyone is. Everyone remembers their own and almost none of anyone else's."**
>
> **"Which means the thing you're still cringing about — they've forgotten. They were busy cringing about theirs."**

### Why this works

The spotlight effect, demonstrated rather than asserted, using the child's own failure to remember as the proof. And the failure is *reassuring* rather than embarrassing, which is a rare and lovely thing to be able to offer a thirteen-year-old.

## 17 · A thread of attention in your body

*The off-screen practice. Ages 3–8 and 9–14.*

### The move — a secret game

> **"Here's a game nobody can see you playing."**
>
> **"Next time you're waiting — in a queue, in the car, in assembly, waiting for someone to stop talking —"**
>
> **"Can you feel your feet? Inside your shoes. Right now."**
>
> **"You can wiggle your toes and nobody will know. That's it. That's the whole game."**
>
> **"Your head can be miles away. Your feet are always right here."**

### Why this works

Secrecy is the hook. Children have very little that is private and entirely theirs, and a game adults can't detect is genuinely appealing in a way that "practise mindfulness" never is. It also targets exactly the moments children find hardest — waiting, transitions, being made to sit still — and it needs no equipment, no screen and no permission.

**Always an invitation.** For some children body attention isn't neutral. "I didn't feel like it today" is a complete answer, every time.

## 18 · The moves that work in the moment

When a child arrives already upset, none of the above applies. No experiments, no images, nothing clever. Short sentences and low demands.

> **"That sounds like a lot."**
>
> **"You don't have to explain it."**
>
> **"Where is it? Just point."**
>
> **"Okay. Stay there a second. I'm not going anywhere."**
>
> **"Is this one for a grown-up?"**

### Why

Teaching requires a settled child. An upset child needs company, not curriculum. **Every clever idea in this document is switched off** when the check-in comes back high-intensity and unpleasant — the app gets quieter and simpler, not smarter. Getting this wrong is how a wellbeing app becomes another thing that doesn't understand.

## Writing rules for anyone adding to this

1. **Let them run it.** If a child can test it in ten seconds, that beats any explanation.
2. **Borrow, don't build.** Use something already in their life — a wobbly tooth, a smoke alarm, the last day of a holiday. Never a metaphor from adult life.
3. **The trapdoor is the best move you have.** An instruction that fails on purpose is delightful, memorable and unarguable.
4. **Stop before the moral.** The instant you add "and that shows us that…", you've turned a discovery into a lesson and lost them.
5. **The child supplies the answer.** Ask questions whose answers come from them. The app's job is the question.
6. **Funny beats profound.** Every single time, at every age.
7. **Nothing is ever wrong.** Not a feeling, not a thought, not a guess, not "I don't know."
8. **When they're upset, none of this applies.** See §18.
