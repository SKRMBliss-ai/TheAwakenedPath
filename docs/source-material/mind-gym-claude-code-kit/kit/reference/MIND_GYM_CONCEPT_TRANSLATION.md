# Mind Gym — Concept Translation Library

**Turning contemplative teaching into original child-friendly content.**

**Owner:** Sim · **Last updated:** 2 September 2026
**Companion to:** `MIND_GYM_MASTER_PLAN.md`
**Sources drawn on conceptually:** Eckhart Tolle (*The Power of Now*, *A New Earth*) · Michael Singer (*The Untethered Soul*, *Living Untethered*) · Shefali Tsabary (*The Awakened Family*)

## 0. How this document was made — read first

This is the audit trail from source idea to original content. If anyone ever asks where a piece of Mind Gym's teaching came from, this is the answer.

### The rules I worked under

These are your own rules from the original project brief, and they're the correct ones:

- **Concepts only.** No reproduction of any source text.
- **No close paraphrase.** Not a sentence rewritten with synonyms.
- **No style imitation.** These authors have distinctive voices. Mind Gym's voice is its own — plain, warm, child-facing.
- **No attribution.** Nothing in the app says "as Eckhart Tolle wrote" or credits a new principle to any author.
- **Non-religious, non-spiritual framing throughout.** These ideas arrive in the app as ordinary psychological skills, because that's what they have to be for a children's product used in schools and mixed-faith homes.

### What that means in practice

Every "child version" below is newly written. The "source idea" column describes the *concept* in my own words — the general insight, of the kind that appears across contemplative traditions and modern psychology alike — not the author's expression of it. Where an idea has a well-established parallel in mainstream psychology, that's noted, because it strengthens the case that we're teaching a skill rather than a doctrine.

### The three-way split

| Source | Nature | Where it lands |
|--------|--------|----------------|
| Singer | Inner life — watching your own mind | **Child path & games** |
| Tolle | Inner life — presence, thought, identity | **Child path & games** |
| Tsabary | Parenting — the adult's own reactivity | **Grown-up guide only** |

*The Awakened Family* is a parenting book. Almost none of it belongs in front of a child. It belongs to the parent-facing material, and it's covered in §4.

### Cluster A — You are the one noticing

The foundation. Everything else rests on a child being able to take a step back from their own experience.

#### A1 · There is a part of you that watches

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Alongside your thoughts and feelings there is something that is *aware* of them. You can notice you're angry, which means "you" and "the anger" aren't the same thing. Singer calls the aware part the seat of consciousness; Tolle points at it as the observer behind thought. |
| **What it means** | Awareness and its contents are separable. This is also, in ordinary psychological terms, the basis of metacognition and of every "step back and look at it" therapy technique. |
| **Child 3–8** | **"You're the one who notices."** *"When you feel cross, there's a you that knows you're cross. That's the noticing you. It's always there, even when the feeling is big."* |
| **Child 9–14** | **"The noticer."** *"You can watch yourself being annoyed. That's odd when you think about it — it means there's a you doing the watching. That you is the part with the choices."* |
| **Lands in** | Step 1 affirmation copy · SH-NAME · the framing of the whole path |
| **Watch for** | Don't imply the child is "not really" their feelings, or that feelings are fake. The feeling is real. There's just also someone noticing it. |

#### A2 · Watching a feeling is different from being lost in it

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | You can be inside a feeling, run by it — or you can observe it while it happens. The second creates room to act differently. |
| **What it means** | Decentering. Well-established in mindfulness-based cognitive therapy. |
| **Child 3–8** | **"Look at it from outside."** *"Imagine the cross feeling is a weather cloud, and you're standing looking up at it. It's still there. But you're not inside it."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"There's a difference between 'I'm furious' and 'I notice I'm furious.' Try saying the second one. Something loosens."* |
| **Lands in** | Step 2 (body) · My Mind Says / I Say · Thought Parade |
| **Watch for** | Never frames as "get out of the feeling" or "don't feel it." Watching is not escaping. |

#### A3 · The noticing part isn't damaged by what it notices

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Whatever storms pass through, the awareness watching them is untouched. |
| **What it means** | For a child, this translates to something plainer and more useful: *you are still you, even on a terrible day.* |
| **Child 3–8** | *"Even on a day when everything goes wrong — you're still you underneath. The you that notices doesn't get broken."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"A bad week doesn't rewrite who you are. The part of you that's watching it happen is the same part that was there last month."* |
| **Lands in** | Reflection Room · after a difficult session · journal framing |
| **Watch for** | Never used to minimise a real problem. This is about identity continuity, not "so it doesn't matter." |

### Cluster B — The mind talks, constantly

#### B1 · There is a voice, and it never stops

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | An almost continuous inner commentary runs in most people's heads — judging, predicting, narrating. Most people never notice it because they're too close to it. Singer's most famous move is inviting you to see it as a separate talker rather than as *you*. |
| **What it means** | Inner speech is a real, well-studied phenomenon. Noticing it is the first metacognitive skill. |
| **Child 3–8** | **"Your brain is a talker."** *"Your brain talks all day long. Sometimes out loud, mostly inside. Have a listen — what's it saying right now?"* |
| **Child 9–14** | **"The commentary."** *"There's a running commentary in your head on basically everything. Most people never notice it's there. Once you do, you can't un-notice it — which turns out to be useful."* |
| **Lands in** | Step 3 · Thought Bubbles · Thought Parade · Thought Room intro |
| **Watch for** | **Tone.** The source material is often sharply comic about how unreasonable the inner voice is. That framing must not survive translation — a child should not be taught that something inside them is broken, mad or an enemy. Curious and friendly, always. |

#### B2 · The voice is not you

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | You can hear the voice, therefore you are not the voice. |
| **What it means** | Cognitive defusion — separating from thought content rather than arguing with it. Core to ACT. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"Your brain says lots of things. You get to decide which ones to listen to. You're the boss, not the talking."* |
| **Child 9–14** | **"My mind says…"** *"Put those three words in front of the thought. 'I'm rubbish at this' becomes 'my mind says I'm rubbish at this.' Same words. Completely different weight."* |
| **Lands in** | My Mind Says / I Say · step 6 framing |
| **Watch for** | Not "your thoughts are lies" and not "ignore it." The move is *distance*, not dismissal or suppression. |

#### B3 · The voice is often wrong, and rarely held to account

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | The inner voice confidently predicts disaster, is proved wrong, and is trusted again immediately. Nobody keeps score. |
| **What it means** | Confirmation bias plus a failure to calibrate. Genuinely useful and very teachable. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"Sometimes your brain guesses. Sometimes the guess is wrong! That's okay — brains guess a lot."* |
| **Child 9–14** | **"Check the record."** *"Your mind was certain everyone would laugh. Did they? Keep score for a week and you'll find out how good your predictions actually are."* |
| **Lands in** | Know or Guess? · Loud Thought Quiet Thought · Looking Back |
| **Watch for** | Never becomes "you're wrong a lot." It's the *prediction* being checked, never the child. |

#### B4 · Loudness isn't truth

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | The most insistent, repetitive inner statements feel the most true and often aren't. |
| **What it means** | Availability and fluency heuristics — familiar, repeated thoughts feel more valid. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"Some thoughts shout. Shouting doesn't make them right."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"The thought that keeps hammering at you isn't the reliable one. It's just the loudest. Those are different things."* |
| **Lands in** | Loud Thought, Quiet Thought |
| **Watch for** | — |

### Cluster C — Thoughts, stories, and what actually happened

The most valuable cluster for this app. Step 5 and step 6 live here.

#### C1 · The event and the story about the event are different things

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Something happens. The mind immediately adds interpretation, meaning and prediction — and the whole bundle is then experienced as "what happened." |
| **What it means** | The cognitive model in one line, and the single most transferable idea in the app. |
| **Child 3–8** | **The camera test.** *"Would a camera see it? 'Sam took the red car' — yes, a camera saw that. 'Sam doesn't like me any more' — no camera can see that. That bit came from your brain."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"What happened, and what you made it mean. Write them in two columns and the gap is usually enormous."* |
| **Lands in** | **Step 5 (the hinge)** · **Step 6 (the turn)** · Camera or Brain? · Detective vs Storyteller |
| **Watch for** | The brain box is **not** the mistake box. Everyone's story has bits in both, and the added bits are often perfectly reasonable. |

#### C2 · More than one story fits the same facts

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Any set of events supports several interpretations. The mind picks one, usually fast, and then treats it as the only one. |
| **What it means** | Cognitive reappraisal — the most evidence-backed emotion-regulation strategy there is. |
| **Child 3–8** | **"Maybe…"** *"Maybe Sam really wanted a turn. Maybe Sam forgot it was yours. Maybe Sam was having a bad day. Same thing happened — three different stories."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"Same facts, different story. Not a nicer story. Just another one that fits everything you actually know."* |
| **Lands in** | **Step 7** · Two Endings · The Maybe Machine · Rewrite the Ending |
| **Watch for** | **Never "the correct story."** The first story isn't a lie. "I can't think of another one yet" must always be a valid answer, or this becomes forced positivity. |

#### C3 · The mind writes to protect you

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Much mental noise is defensive — anticipating threat, guarding a sore spot, bracing for the worst. Singer's image of protecting an old wound rather than letting it heal points at this. |
| **What it means** | Threat-detection bias. The brain is doing its job, badly calibrated. This reframe is what stops the whole exercise becoming self-blame. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"Your brain is trying to keep you safe. Sometimes it worries too much, like a smoke alarm that goes off when you make toast."* |
| **Child 9–14** | **The fear story.** *"That story came from the part of you that's trying to protect you. It assumes the worst so nothing can catch you out. That's not a fault — that's the job. It's just not the only reading."* |
| **Lands in** | **Step 6a (fear/love)** · Fear Story / Love Story · Anger: What's Underneath? |
| **Watch for** | **The most important watch-out in this document.** If a child ever feels their fear story was wrong or bad, they'll stop reporting honestly at step 3 and the whole path hollows out. |

#### C4 · Some thoughts stick, most drift

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Thoughts pass by themselves unless something in us grips them. Attention is what keeps a thought alive. |
| **What it means** | Rumination versus letting a thought complete its arc. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"Some thoughts float away like bubbles. Some stick like glue. Which sort is this one?"* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"Notice which thoughts you keep going back to. The sticky ones aren't more true — they're just the ones you keep feeding."* |
| **Lands in** | Sticky or Floaty? · Thought Parade |
| **Watch for** | Not "make it go away." A child can't unstick a thought by trying. They can notice it's sticky. |

#### C5 · You don't have to follow every thought

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | A thought arriving is not an instruction. |
| **What it means** | Thought–action separation. Underpins impulse control. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"Your brain said 'push him.' You didn't push him. See? A thought isn't the same as doing."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"Having the thought and acting on it are two separate events, with a gap in between. The gap is where you live."* |
| **Lands in** | I Don't Have To Follow It · Pause Room · Freeze Frame |
| **Watch for** | Never implies bad thoughts make a bad person. That's exactly the shame this app must avoid. |

### Cluster D — Feelings move

#### D1 · Feelings come and go on their own

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Emotional states are temporary and self-resolving if not fed. |
| **What it means** | Affective forecasting — people systematically overestimate how long feelings will last. Children especially. |
| **Child 3–8** | **The weather.** *"Feelings are like weather. Storms are loud. Storms also pass. You don't have to do anything to make the weather change."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"You will not feel like this forever. That's not comfort — it's just how feelings work. Check your own journal from three weeks ago."* |
| **Lands in** | Inside Weather · The Feeling Changed · Looking Back · step 8 |
| **Watch for** | Never used to rush a child. "It'll pass" said too early is dismissal. It's an observation offered *after* a feeling has been fully named. |

#### D2 · Pushing a feeling down keeps it

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Resisting an inner experience holds it in place; allowing it lets it move through. |
| **What it means** | Experiential avoidance predicts worse outcomes; acceptance predicts better ones. Well-evidenced. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"You don't have to squash it. Feelings that get squashed just wait around. Let it be there — it'll go on its own."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"Fighting a feeling is how you keep it. Letting it be there — actually letting it — is how it moves."* |
| **Lands in** | Ride the Wave · Sadness: Heavy Things · step 1 affirmation |
| **Watch for** | This is **not** "do nothing about your situation." Feelings get allowed; problems still get solved. See E3. |

#### D3 · Urges rise and fall

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | The pull to act on a feeling peaks and subsides if not acted on. |
| **What it means** | Urge surfing. Established in habit and impulse work. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"The want-to-shout feeling gets big… then smaller… then it's gone. You can just watch it go."* |
| **Child 9–14** | **Ride the wave.** *"The urge crests and drops. You don't have to do anything — you just have to still be there when it passes."* |
| **Lands in** | Ride the Wave · Pause Room · the urge clue |
| **Watch for** | — |

### Cluster E — Now, and what's actually here

#### E1 · Most suffering lives in the past or the future

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Worry projects forward, regret runs backward; the present moment is usually more manageable than either. Tolle's central claim. |
| **What it means** | Rumination and anticipatory anxiety, named plainly. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"Is that happening right now? Or is it a maybe-later thing?"* |
| **Child 9–14** | **Now or not now?** *"Sort your worries into 'happening now' and 'might happen.' Most land in the second pile — and most of that pile never arrives."* |
| **Lands in** | Worry: Now or Not Now? · The What-If Tree |
| **Watch for** | Never "don't think about the future." Planning is good. This is about *distress* about the future, not thought about it. |

#### E2 · The body is a way back to now

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Attention to physical sensation interrupts mental time-travel and returns you to the present. |
| **What it means** | Interoceptive grounding — standard, effective, and already the basis of step 2. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"Feel your feet on the floor. That's happening right now, for real."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"Your body is only ever in the present. When your head is three days ahead, your feet aren't. Start there."* |
| **Lands in** | **Step 2** · Clue Hunt · Jump & Notice · Balloon Breath |
| **Watch for** | Keep it as noticing, never as a breathing technique with force. No breath-holding, no hyperventilation. |

#### E3 · Wanting things to be different is a separate pain from the thing itself ⚠️

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Resisting reality adds a second layer of suffering on top of the first. Acceptance dissolves the second layer. |
| **What it means** | Real and useful for adults. **Severely constrained for children.** |
| **Child 3–8** | *"Some things you can change. Some things you can't, at least not today. It helps to know which is which."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"There's the thing that happened, and there's the fighting-it. The second one is extra, and sometimes you can put it down. That's different from deciding the thing was fine."* |
| **Lands in** | Disappointment: The Gap — **and nowhere else without review** |
| **Watch for** | 🔴 **The highest-risk idea in this document.** Taught carelessly to a child, "accept what is" becomes "put up with it" — and a child being bullied, neglected or hurt must never learn that from us. Every appearance of this concept is paired with: *some things should be changed, and some things you tell a grown-up about.* If in doubt, cut it. |

### Cluster F — Opening and closing

#### F1 · You can close down or stay open

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | In response to hurt, something contracts and shuts. Staying open is a choice, though a hard one. Singer's energetic framing. |
| **What it means** | Approach versus avoidance. Also the ordinary observation that hurt makes people withdraw. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"Sometimes we go all curled up and closed, like a hedgehog. Sometimes we stay open. Both are okay — but notice which one you did."* |
| **Child 9–14** | **Fear and love, as directions.** *"Fear closes things down. Love opens them up. Neither is a rule about how you should feel — they're just two directions a story can point."* |
| **Lands in** | **Step 6a** · Fear Story / Love Story |
| **Watch for** | Closing is protective and sometimes correct. Never taught as a failure. A child who closes down around someone unkind is doing something sensible. |

#### F2 · Guarding a sore spot keeps it sore

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Building your life around not touching an old hurt preserves the hurt. |
| **What it means** | Avoidance maintains distress. |
| **Child 3–8** | *Not taught.* Too abstract, and too close to asking a small child to reopen something painful without support. |
| **Child 9–14** | *"Sometimes we arrange everything around not touching a sore bit. Worth noticing when you're doing that."* — noticing only, never prompting the child to go into it. |
| **Lands in** | My Pattern (older band), lightly |
| **Watch for** | 🔴 The app **never** invites a child toward a painful memory. Anything that surfaces goes to the trusted-adult path. This is a noticing-only concept, and it may be cut entirely without loss. |

#### F3 · Trying to fix the outside so the inside feels okay ⚠️

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Endlessly rearranging external circumstances to manage internal discomfort doesn't work; the discomfort needs addressing directly. |
| **What it means** | True for adults with agency. |
| **Child 3–8** | *Not taught.* |
| **Child 9–14** | *Not taught as stated.* |
| **Lands in** | **Deliberately excluded — see §3.** |
| **Watch for** | 🔴 Children frequently *are* in situations that genuinely need changing, and often lack the power to change them alone. Teaching "the problem is your reaction" to a child is the single most dangerous possible translation in this whole body of work. Excluded outright. |

### Cluster G — Who you are

#### G1 · The picture you carry of yourself isn't all of you

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | We assemble a self-image from others' reactions and then defend it as if it were us. Tolle and Tsabary both point at this. |
| **What it means** | Self-concept — and for children, the crucial fact that it's still forming and isn't fixed. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"You're still finding out who you are. Nobody's finished."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"The version of you in your head was mostly assembled from what other people reflected back. It's a draft, not a verdict."* |
| **Lands in** | Reflection Room · My Pattern · What Would You Tell a Friend? |
| **Watch for** | 🔴 **Do not import "dissolve the ego."** Children are *building* a self, and that's healthy developmental work. The message is "your self-image is still being written," never "your self is an illusion." |

#### G2 · A label isn't a fact

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | Identifying with a description — I'm the anxious one, I'm bad at maths — turns a passing state into a fixed identity. |
| **What it means** | Growth versus fixed mindset, and the difference between state and trait. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"'I felt shy today' is different from 'I'm a shy person.' The first one is what happened. The second one is a label."* |
| **Child 9–14** | *"Watch the difference between 'I messed that up' and 'I'm a mess-up.' One is a fact about an afternoon. The other is a life sentence you handed yourself."* |
| **Lands in** | Camera or Brain? · Detective vs Storyteller · step 6 |
| **Watch for** | — |

#### G3 · You're kinder to your friends than to yourself

| | |
|---|---|
| **Source idea** | The inner voice speaks to us in a way we'd never tolerate from another person, and would never use on someone we love. |
| **What it means** | Self-compassion. Strongly evidenced with adolescents. |
| **Child 3–8** | *"If your friend said that about themselves, what would you say back to them?"* |
| **Child 9–14** | **What Would You Tell a Friend?** *"Your friend has this exact thought about themselves. What do you say? …Now notice you'd never say that to yourself."* |
| **Lands in** | What Would You Tell a Friend? · step 7 alternatives |
| **Watch for** | Best game in the library for this age. Borrows existing kindness rather than lecturing about self-worth. |

## 3. Deliberately not carried across

The most important section. Each of these appears prominently in the source material and is **excluded from the child-facing app**, with reasons.

| Concept | Why it's excluded |
|---------|-------------------|
| **The pain-body** | Teaches that an entity inside you feeds on your suffering. For a child this risks depersonalising their own emotions and creating fear of their inner life. It also edges toward something a distressed child could experience as frightening. Cut entirely. |
| **The inner voice as neurotic / a maniac** | The concept survives (B1); the *tone* does not. A child must never learn that something inside them is deranged. Ours is friendly and curious. |
| **Dissolving the ego / no-self** | Developmentally wrong. Children are constructing a self, and that work is healthy. We teach "your self-image is a draft" (G1) instead. |
| **"The problem is your reaction, not the situation"** | 🔴 Dangerous for children. Many are in situations that genuinely need to change and lack the power to change them alone. Excluded outright, and the app keeps explicit problem-solving and a trusted-adult route as counterweights. |
| **Surrender / total acceptance of what is** | Severely constrained (E3). Never taught as a general principle. A child must never learn to accept mistreatment. |
| **Non-attachment to outcomes** | Reads to a child as "don't care about things." Children should care about things. |
| **Enlightenment, awakening, consciousness, presence as spiritual attainment** | The app is non-religious and used across faiths and none. All framing is ordinary psychological skill. |
| **Meditation as sustained silent practice** | Not age-appropriate as an unsupported instruction. What survives is brief, playful, guided noticing. |
| **Karma, energy centres, past lives, spiritual hierarchy** | Out of scope entirely. |
| **"You are not your thoughts" as a metaphysical claim** | We teach the *practical* version — you can notice a thought and not follow it (B2, C5) — never a claim about the nature of self. |

**The test I applied:** *could a distressed, literal-minded eight-year-old take this the wrong way, and would that be harmful?* If yes, it's either heavily reframed or cut.

## 4. Parent-facing content — from *The Awakened Family*

Tsabary's material is about the adult, and that's where it stays. This is the spine of the grown-up guide, which the app needs anyway (SET-01 to SET-05, SUP-03).

| Concept | Source idea | Parent-facing version |
|---------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Your reaction is yours** | A parent's flare-up is usually about the parent, not the child's behaviour. | *"When your child's behaviour lands hard, the size of your reaction is information about you — your day, your history, your fear. That's not a failure. It's just where the useful work is."* |
| **Fear drives the reaction** | Anger, control and anxiety toward a child are usually fear wearing a different coat. | *"Under most parental anger is a fear: they'll fall behind, they'll be hurt, they'll be judged, I'm doing this wrong. Naming the fear takes the heat out."* |
| **The past arrives in the present** | Old unresolved material gets triggered by a child and lands on them. | *"Sometimes you're not responding to your child. You're responding to something from long before them."* |
| **Reactivity as a trance** | Blind reacting has a trance-like quality you can learn to catch. | *"There's a moment before you react. With practice it gets longer — and Mind Gym is teaching your child exactly that same skill."* |
| **Connection over control** | Controlling behaviour addresses symptoms; connection addresses causes. | *"Getting the behaviour to stop and helping the child are different goals. They sometimes point in opposite directions."* |
| **Children as mirrors** | A child reliably surfaces a parent's unfinished business. | *"The thing your child does that maddens you most is worth a second look. It's often pointing somewhere."* |
| **Growth over perfection** | No parent is conscious all the time; repair matters more than never rupturing. | *"You will lose it. Everyone does. What the child learns from is what happens afterwards."* |
| **The child's own self** | Parenting the child in front of you, not the one you pictured. | *"Your job isn't to make them into anything. It's to keep the conditions right for who they already are."* |

### The one that matters most for this product

**Don't turn Mind Gym into another thing your child has to be good at.**

The fastest way to kill this app is a parent asking "did you do your Mind Gym today?" in the voice they use about homework. That belongs in the welcome screen for grown-ups, stated plainly. The app is not a performance, there is no score, and a parent checking on progress is the beginning of the end of honest answers at step 3.

## 5. Ready-to-use child-facing copy

Drop-in text, written to the voice guide. All original.

### Step 1 — after a feeling is named
- 3–8: *"You found a feeling. That's the whole job — well done."*
- 9–14: *"Named it. That does more than it sounds like it does."*

### Step 2 — the body
- 3–8: *"Some people feel it in their tummy. Some in their chest. Some can't tell at all. Every answer is a real answer."*
- 9–14: *"Your body usually clocks it before your head puts words to it. That head start is the useful bit."*

### Step 3 — the thought
- 3–8: *"What's your brain saying about it? Brains say all sorts. Let's just have a listen."*
- 9–14: *"Whatever it's saying, write it as it actually sounds. No tidying it up."*

### Step 5 — the hinge
- 3–8: *"Look what your brain made! It took what happened and turned it into a whole story."*
- 9–14: *"That's the story. Not the event — the story your mind built on top of it. Worth seeing the difference."*

### Step 6 — the turn
- 3–8: *"Which bits would a camera see? And which bits does only your brain know? Both are allowed."*
- 9–14: *"That story came from the protecting part of you. It's doing its job. It's just not the only reading of what happened."*

### Step 7 — the opening
- 3–8: *"Maybe… maybe… Can you find a different maybe?"*
- 9–14: *"Same facts. What else would fit? Doesn't have to be nicer — just possible."*

### Step 8 — reflection
- 3–8: *"What did you notice? Anything at all counts."*
- 9–14: *"Not what you got right. What you noticed."*

### When the child says "I don't know"
- 3–8: *"That's a real answer. Lots of people don't know. Shall we carry on?"*
- 9–14: *"Fine. 'Don't know' is honest, which is more useful than a made-up answer."*

### When something is too big
- Both: *"This one sounds like it needs a grown-up, not an app. Who could you tell?"*
